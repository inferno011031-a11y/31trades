Trading Journal Template

A trading journal template is a fixed structure for recording every trade the same way, every time. This page shows what a complete template contains, where most spreadsheet templates fall short, and how BattlexJournal turns the same fields into an analytical system.

What a journal template contains

A good template is boring on purpose. It captures the same fields in the same order so that after a few dozen trades you can compare them honestly. The core of every journal template is a trade log with these sections:

1. The trade itself

  • Date and time opened
  • Symbol and direction (long / short)
  • Entry price, exit price, stop loss, take profit
  • Quantity or position size
  • Net P&L and result (win / loss / breakeven)

2. The decision context

  • Setup or pattern that triggered the entry
  • Trading session (London, New York, Asia, etc.)
  • Strategy or system the trade belonged to
  • Timeframe

3. The risk picture

  • Dollar risked on the trade
  • Result in R multiples (P&L ÷ risk)
  • Risk-to-reward ratio at entry

4. The behavior record

  • Emotional state (calm, anxious, revenge-seeking, overconfident)
  • Whether the trade followed your rules
  • Free-text notes: news, context, what you were thinking
  • A post-trade review field: what went well, what to do differently

Most spreadsheet templates stop here — and that is exactly the right place to start. The structure matters more than the tool.

How traders structure their journal

Once the log is consistent, structure helps you extract answers. Three patterns appear in almost every working journal:

  • One row per trade. Everything about a trade lives on a single line so it can be sorted, filtered and totaled.
  • Setup and session as dimensions. These two fields are where most traders find their edge — a setup that wins in London and loses in Asia is invisible unless you record both.
  • A review step, not just a record step. The journal is only complete when each trade (or each day) gets reviewed against the rules. Templates that end at "record the P&L" collect data but produce no learning.

The limitations of spreadsheet templates

Spreadsheets are a fine first journal. They are also where most journals quietly die, for reasons that have nothing to do with your discipline:

  • Manual math. P&L, R multiples and drawdown are easy to get wrong and tedious to maintain. One broken formula and your totals lie to you.
  • No rule evaluation. A spreadsheet records the result of a rule break; it cannot detect the break itself — like exceeding your daily risk limit before you even enter.
  • No connection between records. Your journal, your risk numbers and your discipline score live in different places and drift apart.
  • No natural review loop. Nothing in a spreadsheet reminds you that 14 trades are waiting to be reviewed.
  • Fragile over time. As you trade more, the file grows, the formulas multiply, and eventually the journal becomes a chore instead of a system.

BattlexJournal uses the same fields a good template uses — it just stops treating them as inert columns:
