How to Keep a Trading Journal — The Complete Guide

A trading journal is the single highest-leverage habit a trader can build. This guide covers what to record before, during and after every trade, how to structure the log, how to review it, and how to turn the data into a process that actually improves.

Why a journal works

Trading performance is made of hundreds of small decisions. Memory cannot evaluate those decisions — it remembers the last trade, the biggest win and the most painful loss, and generalizes from all three. A journal replaces memory with a record, and a record is the only thing you can measure, sort and learn from.

The journal does not need to be elaborate to work. It needs to be consistent. Twenty identical rows of data are worth more than fifty beautiful entries with different fields.

Step 1: Choose your fields

Start with the core set that answers "what, when, how much risk, why". You can add fields later, but you cannot reconstruct fields you never recorded.

FieldWhy it matters
Date & timeEnables session and time-of-day analysis.
Symbol & directionThe basics of what you traded and which side.
Entry, exit, stopPrices are facts; memory is not. These make P&L and risk computable.
Risk and RRisk-normalized results — the unit that makes trades comparable.
SetupThe trigger. Without it you cannot measure which setups pay.
SessionOften the biggest performance split in a journal.
StrategyWhich system the trade belonged to.
NotesThe context: news, mood, reasoning at the moment of entry.

Step 2: Record at the moment, not at the end of the week

The value of a note is inversely proportional to how long after the trade you write it. Log the trade when it happens — or at least the same day. If that feels like friction, that friction is the point: it is also why many journals die in week three. Reduce the friction with a journaling tool that calculates the numbers for you, so logging a trade is three fields, not a spreadsheet row of formulas.

Step 3: Review on a schedule

Recording without reviewing is collecting. A working review cadence:

  • Daily (5–10 minutes). Review today's trades against your rules. Mark what followed the plan and what didn't.
  • Weekly (30 minutes). Look at the week's metrics: win rate, profit factor, average R, discipline score. Choose one thing to change.
  • Monthly (1 hour). Evaluate whether your edge still holds by setup, session and strategy — and whether your risk is in balance with your results.

A repeatable review framework is covered in how to review your trades.

Step 4: Separate process from outcome

The hardest part of reviewing is resisting outcome bias. A good decision can lose; a bad decision can win. The journal is the tool that separates the two:

  • Score the decision against your rules and setup — that is process.
  • Record the result — that is outcome.
  • Improve the process using both, without letting a single lucky or unlucky trade rewrite your rules.

Step 5: Turn the record into rules and limits

Once you have a few weeks of consistent records, the journal should start producing decisions, not just data:

  • Set a daily risk budget and loss limit from your actual risk behavior — not a guess.
  • Define the rules your best trades follow, and evaluate every trade against them (this is what discipline scoring automates).
  • Cut the setups and sessions your analytics show are losing, and scale the ones that win.

Tools: spreadsheet vs. a journaling system

A spreadsheet is a perfectly good starting journal — many traders log their first hundred trades in one. Its limits appear later: manual math, no rule evaluation, no connection to risk and analytics, and a review loop that depends on your own reminders. That is where a dedicated journal takes over. The trade journal import moves an existing spreadsheet history into BattlexJournal with validation, so switching does not mean starting over.

Common mistakes

Most journal failures are avoidable: inconsistent fields, logging only losses, skipping reviews, judging trades by P&L alone, and quitting during the first losing week. The detailed list, with fixes, is in 7 trading journal mistakes.
